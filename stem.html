Environmental & Digital Projects

GRAPEVINE — Environmental Events Australia

GRAPEVINE is a website I designed and developed to make it easier for people across Australia to discover environmental, nature and community events in their local area.

I created the project because many small, free and low-cost environmental activities can be difficult to discover when information is spread across councils, community organisations and individual event pages.

The website brings these events together into one place and allows organisers and community members to contribute events. I developed the project myself, from the original concept through to the functioning website.

The project combines my interests in environmental education, community engagement and digital technology, while giving me practical experience in web development, information organisation, user experience and maintaining a live website.

Type: Web Development / Environmental Project Focus: Environment / Community / Digital Technology Technologies: HTML / CSS / JavaScript Status: Live Project

Repair & Reuse

I enjoy finding discarded technology and household electronics in hard rubbish and exploring whether they can be repaired, reused or repurposed rather than thrown away.

This has involved repairing damaged cords, troubleshooting equipment and working out how unfamiliar devices function. I enjoy the process of identifying a problem, experimenting with possible solutions and finding a practical way to give something a second life.

Focus: Repair / Reuse / Problem Solving Approach: Hands-on experimentation

Science & Experimentation

Growing Sesame Seeds — An Experiment in Seed Viability

I became interested in growing sesame after learning that the CSIRO was encouraging Australian farmers to explore growing sesame domestically. The COVID-19 pandemic had highlighted vulnerabilities in Australia's supply chains, including the importance of having greater diversity in Australian agricultural production.

I contacted the CSIRO to ask whether they could provide sesame seeds for experimentation. Their seed repository was focused on Australian tree species, so I began looking for other sources.

I tried to purchase seeds within Australia, but finding viable seeds proved surprisingly difficult. I also discovered that importing sesame seeds from overseas was not permitted.

I then began contacting Australian stores to find untreated sesame seeds that might still be capable of germinating. Several organic stores believed they had suitable seeds, but when I tested them, they were not viable.

I decided to systematically test the different packets of sesame seeds I could find. I prepared agar in a petri dish and used a template underneath the dish to create nine evenly spaced positions.

I carefully placed nine seeds from each packet onto the agar and repeated the experiment across five rounds of testing.

Eventually, one of the samples germinated. I found that the viable sample in my testing was black sesame seed that retained its outer seed coat.

I then researched the conditions required to grow sesame and successfully grew the plants during the summer.

What began as a question about whether store-bought sesame seeds could be grown became a small investigation involving research, experimentation, observation, persistence and problem solving.

Type: Independent Experiment Focus: Botany / Agriculture / Seed Germination Methods: Research / Observation / Experimentation Outcome: Successfully germinated and grew sesame
